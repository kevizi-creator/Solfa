Tips

How to name a note

  1. Stable or tense? Do, Mi and So feel settled. Re, Fa, La and Ti feel tense and lean toward a settled note. That leaves you two or three choices.
  2. Walk to it. After you hear Do and the mystery note, sing Do, Re, Mi in your head until you land on the note you heard.
  3. Sing it out loud right after you hear it. It sticks better than listening alone.
  4. Link notes to tunes. The first two notes of "Twinkle, Twinkle" are Do and So. Use songs from your own choir.
  5. Build up slowly. Begin at Starter, then move up a level when you score well.
  6. Hear minor. Ri, Si and Li sound darker than Mi, La and Ti. Use Hear scale to compare the major and minor scales.
  7. Short and often. Ten minutes a day beats one long session.

Why train your ear

  • Pass the choir test: hear Do in any key, then name or sing any note.
  • Sing in tune. Your voice learns to find the note you hear in your head.
  • Learn new songs faster by ear, and read music more easily.
  • Blend with the choir. You notice sooner when you drift flat or sharp.
  • Keep your own part when others sing different harmony notes around you.

How long will ear training take?

With about 10 minutes a day, most people name Do, Mi and So reliably in 1 to 2 weeks. The full major scale usually takes 4 to 8 weeks, and the in-between notes (Di, Ri, Fi, Si, Li) and all 12 keys about 2 to 3 months. Everyone is different. Move up a level when you score well, not before.

Breath control

  1. Breathe low. Let your belly and lower ribs widen as you breathe in. Keep your shoulders down and still.
  2. Let the air out slowly, as if blowing through a straw. Try to make the out-breath longer than the in-breath.
  3. Challenge. The out-breath grows by 1 s every 3 breaths. Tap Too long, go back whenever it stops being easy.
  4. Hold, do not push. A steady, easy note lasts longer than a loud one. Stop before you strain.

A common myth about breath control

Myth"The longer I can hold my breath, the better my breath control."
The truthHolding your breath trains your body to keep air in. Singing needs the opposite: a slow, steady stream of air going out. A singer is not judged by how long they can hold air, but by how evenly they can let it go.

What to do instead

  • Breathe in low and easy. Let your belly and lower ribs widen. Do not gulp air or lift your shoulders.
  • Make the out-breath long and even, like a soft, steady "sss" or blowing through a straw. Build it up a little at a time with the Challenge.
  • Keep the flow steady on the note. Start gently. If you push a lot of air at the start, you run out too soon.
  • Test it by singing. How long you can hold a note steadily on pitch is the real measure, so use Hold note.
  • Stay relaxed. Stop if you feel strain or dizziness.

Why train your breath

  • Longer phrases without gasping for air halfway through.
  • Steadier notes. Even air means less wobble, so it is easier to stay on pitch.
  • A fuller, clearer tone and easier control of how loud or soft you sing.
  • Less strain on your throat, because your breath does the work.
  • Calmer nerves on test day. Slow breathing settles you.

How long will breath training take?

Most people notice an easier, longer out-breath after about 2 to 3 weeks of 5 to 10 minutes a day. Steadier held notes and longer phrases usually follow within 1 to 2 months. Everyone is different, so go by how it feels, and keep it easy and regular instead of forcing it.
